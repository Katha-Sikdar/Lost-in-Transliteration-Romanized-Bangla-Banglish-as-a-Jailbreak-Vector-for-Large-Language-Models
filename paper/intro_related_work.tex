\section{Introduction}

Large language models (LLMs) are now deployed as general-purpose assistants
to hundreds of millions of users worldwide. To prevent misuse, model developers
align these systems with human preferences through supervised fine-tuning and
reinforcement learning from human feedback
\citep{ouyang2022training, bai2022constitutional}, and further protect them with
input--output guard models \citep{inan2023llamaguard, zeng2024shieldgemma}.
Despite these efforts, a growing body of work shows that safety alignment can
be circumvented by \emph{jailbreak} attacks, ranging from hand-crafted
role-play prompts \citep{shen2024anything} and persuasion
\citep{zeng2024johnny} to automated optimization
\citep{zou2023universal, chao2023jailbreaking, mehrotra2024tree}.
\citet{wei2023jailbroken} attribute many of these failures to
\emph{mismatched generalization}: a model's capabilities extend to input
distributions that its safety training never covered.

Language is one of the most consequential such distributions. Safety training
data is overwhelmingly in English, and prior work has shown that simply
translating harmful requests into low-resource languages substantially
increases attack success rates
\citep{yong2023lowresource, deng2024multilingual, wang2024languages}.
However, existing multilingual safety benchmarks share a common assumption:
each language is represented in its \emph{native script}, usually obtained by
machine-translating English prompts. This assumption does not reflect how a
large fraction of the world actually types. Speakers of many South Asian
languages routinely write their language in the Latin alphabet, a practice
known as \emph{romanization} \citep{roark2020dakshina}.

Bangla (Bengali) is a prominent example. With \todo{over 230 million native
speakers; cite Ethnologue or a census source}, it is among the most widely
spoken languages in the world, yet it remains under-resourced in NLP
\citep{joshi2020state, bhattacharjee2022banglabert}. In everyday digital
communication---social media, messaging apps and, increasingly, chatbots---Bangla
speakers frequently write in \emph{Banglish}: romanized Bangla with no
standardized orthography, often mixed with English words
(e.g., \textit{``tumi kemon acho?''} or \textit{``tmi kmn aso?''} for
``How are you?''). Banglish therefore sits at an unusual intersection: its
surface form is Latin-script and shares tokens with English, while its
underlying language is a low-resource one that safety training rarely covers.

We hypothesize that this intersection creates a distinct safety blind spot.
Because Banglish is tokenized much like English, capable LLMs may
\emph{understand} romanized harmful requests well enough to comply, while
their safety mechanisms---tuned on English and on native-script data---fail to
\emph{recognize} those requests as harmful. Moreover, the absence of a
standard spelling means that a single request admits many surface variants,
each of which may evade keyword- or classifier-based filters differently.
To our knowledge, no prior work has systematically evaluated LLM safety on
romanized Bangla.

A key methodological challenge is distinguishing a genuine jailbreak from a
comprehension failure: a model that fails to refuse may simply have
misunderstood the input. Prior multilingual evaluations rarely control for
this. We therefore score every response along two axes---whether the model
understood the request, and whether its response was harmful---and count only
on-topic harmful responses as successful attacks.

\begin{enumerate}

    region-specific harms such as communal incitement, election
    misinformation and mobile-financial-service fraud, together with benign
    borderline prompts for measuring over-refusal.

    \item A large-scale evaluation of \todo{M} proprietary and open-weight
    LLMs and \todo{G} guard models. We find that \todo{summarize headline
    result, e.g., ``attack success rates on noisy Banglish are X$\times$

We release BanglishJail under gated access to support research on
romanized-language safety, and we disclosed our findings to the affected

methodology directly applies to other widely romanized languages such as
Hindi (Hinglish), Urdu and Arabic (Arabizi).

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Jailbreak attacks on aligned LLMs.}
Early jailbreaks relied on manually crafted prompts such as role-play and
``Do Anything Now'' templates, which \citet{shen2024anything} collected and
characterized at scale. \citet{wei2023jailbroken} proposed two failure modes
of safety training---competing objectives and mismatched
generalization---the latter of which motivates our study.
Subsequent work automated jailbreak discovery: GCG \citep{zou2023universal}
optimizes adversarial suffixes with gradients, PAIR \citep{chao2023jailbreaking}
and TAP \citep{mehrotra2024tree} use an attacker LLM to iteratively refine
prompts in a black-box setting, persuasive adversarial prompts
\citep{zeng2024johnny} exploit social-science persuasion techniques, and
many-shot jailbreaking \citep{anil2024manyshot} exploits long context windows.
Standardized benchmarks such as HarmBench \citep{mazeika2024harmbench} and
JailbreakBench \citep{chao2024jailbreakbench} enable consistent comparison,
but are English-only. Our work is complementary: rather than proposing a new
optimization procedure, we study a \emph{naturally occurring} input
distribution that existing defenses do not cover.

\paragraph{Multilingual LLM safety.}
\citet{yong2023lowresource} showed that translating unsafe English prompts
into low-resource languages such as Zulu and Scots Gaelic bypasses GPT-4's
safeguards. \citet{deng2024multilingual} introduced MultiJail, a benchmark
spanning nine non-English languages including Bengali, and found that unsafe
output rates increase as language resource availability decreases.
\citet{wang2024languages} proposed XSafety, a multilingual safety benchmark
covering \todo{verify number and list of languages}, and
\citet{shen2024language} analyzed why safety degrades in non-English contexts.
\citet{aakanksha2024multilingual} collected human-written multilingual
red-teaming prompts and studied the trade-off between global and local harm
mitigation. All of these works represent each language in its native script,
predominantly via machine translation of English prompts. In contrast, we
study the romanized form in which Bangla is commonly written online, use
native-authored rather than translated prompts, and include
culturally specific harm categories.

\paragraph{Code-switching and romanized text.}
Code-mixing is pervasive in multilingual communities and has long challenged
NLP systems \citep{khanuja2020gluecos}. Recent work on code-switching
red-teaming \citep{yoo2024codeswitching} % VERIFY venue and authors
shows that mixing languages within a prompt can elicit unsafe behavior.
Romanization is a related but distinct phenomenon: the language does not
change, only the script, and spelling varies widely across writers.
Resources such as the Dakshina dataset \citep{roark2020dakshina} and the
Aksharantar corpus with IndicXlit models \citep{madhani2023aksharantar}
support transliteration between romanized and native scripts for South Asian
languages, including Bangla. We use such resources both to analyze spelling
variation and to construct a back-transliteration defense. To our knowledge,
the safety implications of romanized Bangla have not been studied.

\paragraph{Bangla NLP.}
Despite its large speaker population, Bangla is classified as a
low-resource language \citep{joshi2020state}. Prior work has developed
pretrained models such as BanglaBERT \citep{bhattacharjee2022banglabert} and
datasets for tasks such as \todo{cite Bangla hate-speech / sentiment /
Banglish datasets}. However, safety evaluation of generative LLMs in
Bangla---and in Banglish in particular---remains largely unexplored.

\paragraph{Defenses and the mechanics of refusal.}
Defenses against jailbreaks include guard models such as Llama Guard
\citep{inan2023llamaguard} and ShieldGemma \citep{zeng2024shieldgemma},
input perturbation methods such as SmoothLLM \citep{robey2023smoothllm},
and classifier-based safeguards \citep{sharma2025constitutional}. Most guard
models officially support a limited set of languages
\todo{verify which languages each guard model lists}.
On the mechanistic side, \citet{arditi2024refusal} showed that refusal in
chat models is mediated by a single direction in the residual stream, and
\citet{qi2025safety} argued that current safety alignment is
``shallow,'' concentrated in the first few output tokens. \citet{qi2024finetuning}
further showed that alignment is fragile under fine-tuning. We build on
these insights to explain why safety fails to transfer to romanized
inputs and to design targeted defenses.
